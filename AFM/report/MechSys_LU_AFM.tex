\chapter{Abstract}

In this lab exercise, a scaled-up model of an atomic force microscope (AFM) is analyzed using two predominant operation modes: contact mode and tapping mode.
The strain gauge sensor and lorentz actuator are first calibrated and characterized, then force curves are recorded to display the basic working principle of the AFM.
Finally, an integral controller is used to control the tip-sample distance to record the sample topography and analyze the frequency response of the system in both operation modes.

\chapter{Setup}

\bilder{htb}{1}{images/setup}{}{1}{images/setup_schematics}{}{Setup of AFM experiments and schematics}{fig:afm_setup}

In fig.~\ref{fig:afm_setup}, the setup of the AFM experiments is shown. Depending on the experiment, different parts of the setup are used.
The sample is placed on a stage that can be manually moved vertically to align with the AFM tip and horizontally to scan the sample surface.
Therefore, we can only make linear measurements (not 2D) of the topography.
The AFM consists of a cantilever with a plastic tip (round or sharp) that can be vertically actuated by a Lorentz actuator.
To measure the deflection of the cantilever, four strain gauges are attached to the cantilever, which are connected in a Wheatstone bridge configuration.
Some initial offset can be compensated with a potentiometer.
The deflection signal can then be optionally filtered with a low-pass filter (not shown in the image) before it is fed into an integral controller, which
compares it to a given set-point and feeds back to the Lorentz actuator.

In the subsequent experiments, only the parts of the setup that are labeled in the corresponding exercise are used.

Only the power supply and sensor readout is connected (see fig.~\ref{fig:afm_setup}). By turning the small potentiometer on the readout electronics board, the offset is adjusted until the sensor output reads zero

\subsection*{2.}

\bilder{h}{1}{images/2_lorentz_characterization}{}{1}{images/2_lorentz_characterization_schematics}{}{Lorentz actuator characterization setup and schematics}{fig:lorentz_setup}

To characterize the Lorentz actuator we use the sample stage as a measurement reference and set it to an arbitrary height below the minimum AFM tip height (see fig.~\ref{fig:lorentz_setup} for schematics).
We apply a voltage in $\qty{100}{\mV}$ steps to the actuator and measure the distance from the cantilever to the sample with a caliper (see fig.~\ref{fig:lorentz_characterization}a).
By plotting the measured distance over the applied voltage (see fig.~\ref{fig:lorentz_characterization}b), we can clearly see a linear relationship where the height increases by about $\qty{0.27}{\mm\per\mV}$.

This linear relationship is beacause of
$$\begin{aligned}
F_\mathrm{L} &= N I B l &\propto I \qquad \text{Lorentz Force}\\
F_\mathrm{L} &= k \Delta z &\propto \Delta z \qquad \text{Hooke's Law}\\
\end{aligned}$$

\bilder{h}{1}{images/IMG_6045}{}{1}{images/2_lorentz_characterization_chart}{}{Lorentz actuator characterization measurement}{fig:lorentz_characterization}

\subsection*{3.}

\bilder{h}{1}{images/2_lorentz_characterization}{}{1}{images/3_plant_sweep}{}{Plant characterization setup and schematics}{fig:plant_sweep}

Next, a sine sweep from $\qty{10}{\Hz}$ to $\qty{100}{\Hz}$ is applied to the Lorentz actuator and the frequency response of the free cantilever is measured with an oscilloscope (see fig.~\ref{fig:plant_sweep}).
The resulting Bode plot is shown in fig.~\ref{fig:plant_sweep_bode}.
We start at $-180^{\circ}$ phase lag due to the inertia of the cantilever. The plot shows two dominant resonances at $\qty{18.9}{\Hz}$ and $\qty{22.1}{\Hz}$, adding $-360^{\circ}$ of phase lag in total, as well as some structural modes at higher frequencies.

\section{Force Curve}

\subsection*{4.}

\bilder{h}{1}{images/4_force_curve_setup}{}{1}{images/4_force_curve_schematics}{}{Force curve measurement setup and schematics}{fig:force_curve_setup}

Next, the force curve is measured by applying a slow triangular wave to the Lorentz actuator and measuring the cantilever deflection with the oscilloscope (see fig.~\ref{fig:force_curve_setup}).
The force curve can be used to determine properties of the sample surface. In this case, the qualitative difference between a sticky and non-sticky surface.
The results are shown in fig.~\ref{fig:force_curve}. The force curve of the sticky surface shows oscillations caused by the adhesion forces after releasing the tip from the surface.

Fig.~\ref{fig:force_curve_AFM} shows the force curve of a real AFM. At atomic level, adhesive and repulsive forces are always present and visible in the force curve.
The pull-off effect shown in the blue curve due to the adhesive forces is similar to the one observed in the experiment with the sticky surface.
However, this effect becomes much too small to be observed with the non-sticky surface.

\subsection*{6.}

\bilder{h}{1}{images/6_controller}{}{1}{images/6_controller_schematics}{}{Controller implementation setup and schematics}{fig:controller}

The integrator itself has a gain of $K_\mathrm{I} = \frac{1}{RC} = \qty{45.5}{\per\s} = \qty{7.2}{\Hz}$ using the nominal values $R=\qty{220}{\kilo\ohm}$ and $C=\qty{100}{\nano\F}$.
If the rest of the system is very fast, this would be the bandwidth of the closed-loop system. However, the bandwidth of the whole system is limited by the mechanical resonances of the cantilever,
therefore we must expect a realistic bandwidth of about 1 order of magnitude lower than the integrator bandwidth, i.e. around $\qty{0.7}{\Hz}$.

The setpoint is set to a constant value in order to set the controller output to zero when the cantilever is touching the sample surface at the neutral position.

\subsection*{11.}

Next, the sensor is supplied with an oscillating voltage at the same frequency $\omega$ as the actuator but with a phase offset $\phi$ relative to the actuating voltage in order to compensate 
the phase offset of the mechanical system. For illustrative purposes, we assume that the phase offset of the mechanical system relative to the actuation is 0.
The sample height limits the cantilever movement to some amplitude $A$.
The readout voltage is given by:
$$U_{out} = U_{sens} \cdot U_{act} \propto A \cdot \cos(\omega t + \phi) \cdot \cos(\omega t) \propto A \cdot ( \cos(\phi) + \cos(2\omega t + \phi) )$$
This yields a signal with an AC component of double the actuation frequency and a DC component which depends on the phase offset.

By low-pass filtering out just the DC component, we get:
$$U_{out} \propto A \cdot \cos(\phi)$$

This means that ideally, the output voltage is a DC signal which is directly proportional to the sample height with a sensitivity given by the adjustable phase offset $\phi$, which should be $n\pi$
for maximum readout.
